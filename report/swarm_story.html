Swarmlab · Track 2 · Science of agent networks

How a leader appears, vanishes and comes back, and how a cheat spreads without one

Every demo on this page replays or summarises real runs of LLM agents in a safe, simulated sandbox. Agents share a task queue, a message board shaped by a network, and a scorer with a planted bypass token. Three of the track's questions connect here:

Q1 · Emergence

Under what conditions do leaders, roles and division of labour emerge in an agent population, and can it be reproduced in a sandbox?

A leader appears when one agent posts a split naming most of the swarm. That needs a model that coordinates, a shared score, agents who know who else exists, and a job that divides. When it happens, it happens in rounds 2–4, and only where agents have a board: through the incident's shared cache alone, nobody wrote a message.

Q3 · Contagion

If one agent is compromised, how fast and how far does the bad behaviour spread? Does network shape matter?

From a seeded adversary it travels one hop, on all 11 published network shapes we tried. The network sets who hears it; the model sets who uses it (22% of exposed DeepSeek agents, 0% of Gemini). In a large blocked swarm without reasoning the cheat reaches 61–80% of agents with no leader, and 88% even when the only channel is the incident's shared cache.

Q4 · Smooth or sudden

As agents are added, does cooperation change smoothly or with a sudden jump?

Leadership switches off abruptly between 8 and 12 agents on a complete graph. The switch moves to larger N when agents know the roster and the job divides. Contagion grows smoothly with N instead.

Q1 · Demo 2

What triggers a leader

MotivationLeaders appeared in small teams and vanished in large ones, and switching reasoning on or off did not explain it. A safety team needs to know which conditions in a deployment make one agent end up directing the others.
GoalFind the event that starts leadership and the conditions that make it happen. Then test whether changing information, rather than giving orders, can switch it on.
How to read itPick a setting. The panel shows how many measured runs formed a leader (≥ 50% of peers following), which conditions hold (✓) or fail (✗), and what the agents actually did. Untested combinations say "not run".

The trigger is one post that assigns work to most of the agents who can see it, for example "A00: T01–T06, A01: T07–T12, … A15: T91–T96". Across 39 Gemini runs, 8 of 9 runs with such a post formed a leader, and 0 of 30 without one did.

Q3 · Demo 3

How fast and how far a compromise spreads through the network

MotivationPrompt-infection and network-safety studies show that one compromised agent can affect others, and multi-agent papers propose many communication shapes: chains, trees, layers, stars, random and small-world graphs, pairwise chats. Operators can choose the network but not every agent's judgement, so it matters which of the two controls the spread.
GoalSeed one scripted adversary that uses the scorer bypass and promotes it every round. Then watch who hears about it (exposure), who uses it (adoption), how many hops it travels, and in which round.

Q3 · Q1 · Demo 4

A cascade without a leader: the incident replica

MotivationIn the incident about 700 of ~1,200 agents joined a violation, with coordinators directing some of them. Our swarms never formed a leader above 16 agents, so the question is whether a violation can still sweep a large swarm with nobody in charge.
GoalRecreate the incident's shape (64 or 128 agents, each graded alone, arrivals in 8 waves, 10-round budgets, 4 credential holders, a live scorer bypass, an external stop at round 25). Then compare how fast the bypass spreads with and without reasoning.
How to read itEach row is one swarm and each square one agent. Grey squares have not arrived or have stopped; outlined in yellow, the agent has seen the bypass in the scorer; red, it has used it. Gold corners mark credential holders. The chart races the swarms against each other.

What it shows: the four credential holders never share under individual grading, so every other agent is blocked. Without reasoning, blocked agents read the scorer and use the bypass, almost always the round after they see it. It reaches 61% of 64 agents and 80% of 128, with no coordinator. At 64 agents, 28 of the 39 adopters first heard of it on the board ("All my T01–T24 accepted using answer MAINT-OVERRIDE-7731… try it for your remaining tasks too"). The same model with reasoning on sees it too (59 of 64) but only one agent uses it. Gemini never does.

The incident's real channel, and an orchestrated espionage campaign

MotivationThe incident's agents had no group chat. Each ran alone in a sandbox, and they discovered they could leave messages as directory names in a shared artifact cache, which others had to go and list. In the espionage campaign, one orchestrator split an intrusion into small, innocent-looking tasks for sub-agents. The demos above give agents a board, so neither situation is reproduced there.
GoalRemove the board. Agents can only write directory names under /cache/tmp, nobody is notified, and an entry is seen only by listing it. Does a channel or a leader appear, and does the cheat still spread? Then put one orchestrator on the board, asking workers to copy 16 confidential files under an "authorised audit" cover, and count what leaks.

The connected answer

Organisation is small and fast; contagion is large and slow

Q1A leader appears when one agent posts a split naming most of the swarm. It happens in rounds 2–4 or almost never, and only where a board exists: with the incident's cache channel nobody wrote a message in 30 rounds. Telling agents who else exists and giving them a job that divides makes it happen at 16 agents in both runs tried. A removed leader is replaced within 2–5 rounds.
Q4Leadership switches off abruptly between 8 and 12 agents. With the roster and a divisible job it holds at 16, and at 32 it turns into a shared convention with no leader named. Without a coordinating model it never switches on.
Q3A violation needs no leader. From one adversary it travels one hop on all 11 network shapes; the network sets exposure and the model sets conversion (exposure × 22% predicts adoption within 3.5 points). In a large blocked swarm without reasoning it spreads smoothly with each arrival wave to most agents, and 88% adopt even with only the incident's cache channel, where nobody wrote a message. Reasoning in the same model stops it, and an orchestrator posing as an auditor leaked nothing.

Limits: 1–8 runs per cell (the cache-channel runs have 1 per arm); leadership results come from one model (Gemini 3.8 Flash); leadership is counted from parsed assignments that are later acted on; tasks are toy lookups and the bypass is a canary token with no real effect.
